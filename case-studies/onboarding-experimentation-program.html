Case study

A/B testing for customer onboarding

A B2B payments fintech ran 18+ A/B tests on its new-customer onboarding funnel. I built the data platform they ran on: pipelines landing LaunchDarkly assignment data and product events in the data lake through Fivetran and Segment, dbt models turning exposures and outcomes into experiment readouts, and the templated Tableau framework the program reported from.

Context

The onboarding funnel

The platform automates accounts payable (AP: the bills a business owes its vendors) and accounts receivable (AR: the invoices it is owed). New customers start in a trial. They connect a bank account, add vendors, and pay a first bill on the AP side or send a first invoice on the AR side. Paying a first bill within 30 days is the strongest predictor of paid conversion and retention, so onboarding is designed to get customers to that action.

Growth owned the funnel. Analytics and Data Science defined the experimentation standards. Every proposed change to onboarding, from task ordering to payment prompts to network discovery, was judged on two agreed metrics: activation (a customer completing the first core money movement within 7 or 30 days) and paid conversion (a trial customer becoming a paying subscriber).

Measuring that many concurrent tests reliably is a data problem before it is a statistics problem. Assignment data, behavioral events, and revenue outcomes lived in different systems; each experiment needed them joined the same way, checked the same way, and reported the same way. That platform is what this case study covers.

Architecture

How experiment data reached the lake

Experiments were assigned and served with LaunchDarkly feature flags: targeting rules split eligible customers into control and treatment, and every flag evaluation produced an exposure event. Two managed pipelines carried everything downstream. Segment routed exposure and product events from the web and mobile apps to the data lake and to the product-analytics tools (Mixpanel for funnels, Pendo for in-app guides). Fivetran synced the structured side on a schedule: LaunchDarkly flag and experiment definitions, plus Mixpanel and Pendo exports, landing as raw schemas next to the event stream.

The platform

Three layers, one contract

The build sits on the diagram above as three layers. Together they guarantee one thing: every experiment is measured from the same tables, by the same definitions, on the same schedule.

1

Ingestion

Airflow-scheduled loads land both paths in the lake: Segment events and Fivetran-synced configs. Identity stitching links anonymous visitor to admin user to customer account, so an exposure on the marketing site and a bill paid in the product resolve to the same customer. Raw flag evaluations become deduplicated assignment tables, one row per customer per experiment, attributed to first exposure, with sample-ratio-mismatch checks on every refresh to catch broken randomization early.

2

Metrics

Activation, conversion, verification, and segment definitions (including ICP, ideal customer profile: the customers the product is built and priced for) are modeled once as dbt marts. Experiment readout models join the same exposure tables to the same metric tables for every test, and compute the statistics in the model rather than in ad hoc queries. Two analysts asking the same question get the same number.

3

Reporting

A templated Tableau framework: registering an experiment's metadata produces a complete dashboard with arms, lift, confidence intervals, segment cuts, and guardrails. No per-experiment workbook building; readout setup went from days to hours.

Method

How an A/B test works

An A/B test is a randomized controlled experiment. Every experiment in this program followed the same six steps.

1

Write the hypothesis first

A written statement before launch: the change, the primary metric it should move, guardrail metrics that must not degrade, and the segments to read (ICP versus all customers).

2

Commit to a sample size

Start from the MDE (minimum detectable effect: the smallest lift worth acting on) and compute the customers needed per arm at the chosen confidence and power:

n per arm  =  (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2 · [p1(1−p1) + p2(1−p2)](p2 − p1)2

At this funnel's scale: detecting a 1.0 pp lift on a 5.5% baseline first-bill rate, at 95% confidence and 80% power ((1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.84), needs about 8,800 customers per arm, roughly two to three weeks of trial volume.

3

Randomize customers into arms

Eligible customers are split randomly into control (current experience) and treatment (the change). Randomization is what makes the result causal: both groups see the same seasonality, marketing, and bugs, so a difference in outcomes is attributable to the change itself.

4

Run to the committed horizon

The test runs until the pre-committed sample size is reached. No stopping early because the p-value looks good on a given day; that practice inflates false positives. Guardrail metrics are monitored throughout.

5

Read out with a z-test

For conversion-style metrics the readout is a two-proportion z-test. With p̂T, p̂C the observed rates and nT, nC the group sizes:

z  =  p̂T − p̂C√p̄(1−p̄) (1/nT + 1/nC)  ,   p̄ = xT + xCnT + nC

Under the null hypothesis H0: pT = pC, the statistic is approximately standard normal, so |z| ≥ 1.96 means the lift is significant at α = 0.05. Every reported lift carries its 95% confidence interval: (p̂T − p̂C) ± 1.96 · SE.

6

Convert to dollars, then decide

Statistical significance is not a ship decision. Each concluded experiment was converted to estimated annual impact (lift × addressable volume) before rollout. A statistically significant 0.2 pp lift can still be a no-ship.

Operating model

How an experiment ran, design to rollout

The statistics above are the contract; this is the process that enforced it across teams.

1

Design review

The owner filed the hypothesis; Analytics and Data Science signed off on the primary metric, guardrails, segment definitions, and the sample-size commitment before any code was written.

2

Instrumentation

The feature flag and targeting rules were created in LaunchDarkly; exposure events were wired through Segment and validated in staging. The rule: events land correctly in the lake before launch, not after.

3

Ramped launch

Traffic ramped from a small canary to the full 50/50 split. The sample-ratio-mismatch check ran from day one; the flag itself was the kill switch.

4

Monitoring

The templated Tableau readout refreshed daily with guardrails alongside the primary metric. A weekly checkpoint with the Growth team reviewed health, not results; results stayed sealed until the horizon.

5

Readout and decision

At the committed horizon: z-test, confidence intervals, segment cuts, and the dollarized impact estimate, presented at the program review for a ship or no-ship call.

6

Rollout

A win went to 100% of its segment through the same flag, the losing code path was removed, and the result was logged. The rolled-out experience became the new control for the next test, so wins compounded without double-counting lift.

Results

Representative experiments

Prompt to pay a first bill CONCLUDED · WON

Roughly one in eighteen new AP customers paid a vendor in their first 30 days, even though that action is the strongest predictor of conversion and retention. The test: after a key setup action (adding a vendor, creating a bill, adding a payment method), prompt the admin with an explicit “pay your first bill” task.

  • +92% 7-day activation and +21% 30-day activation in the ICP segment
  • ≈ $5.6M estimated annual impact; rolled into the successor onboarding flow

Payments-network discovery CONCLUDED · WON

For entry-tier (Essentials) customers, onboarding added education about the platform’s payments network. Paying a vendor who is already on the network requires no vendor setup, so discovery shortens the path to a first payment.

  • +12% activation and +17% paid conversion for Essentials customers
  • ≈ $4.1M projected annual incremental LTV (customer lifetime value); rolled out to 100% of the segment

Credit line application in advanced onboarding CONCLUDED · WON

Advanced-trial customers who signaled interest in financing (a revolving credit line for paying bills) previously had their application parked after sign-up. The tested flow carried the credit line application forward as part of onboarding itself.

  • +55% credit line application starts and +52% submitted applications
  • +15% 30-day activation and +6% paid conversion; ≈ $2.9M estimated annual impact

Retrospective

What aged out

Flag-based assignment with Fivetran-synced configs and Segment-routed exposures was the right answer in 2022. Today I would use warehouse-native experimentation (GrowthBook or Eppo) on a dbt metrics layer, and add CUPED variance reduction to shorten the two-to-three-week runtimes. The spreadsheet-linked deep dives that supplemented the dashboards would be the first thing to retire: the analyses were sound, but recurring analysis belongs in governed marts, not linked spreadsheets. Fixed-horizon testing served the program well; a sequential (always-valid) testing framework is the natural next step.
